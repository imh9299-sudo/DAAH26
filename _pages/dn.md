## Digital Literacy Reflection: A Visual Arts Student's Journey

As a twenty-one-year-old Visual Arts major with a minor in Creative Writing, I find myself discovering the ways in which artistic practice is deeply entangled with digital systems. Over this  course, my understanding of digital literacies has shifted significantly. What I once believed were just a set of technical skills, like knowing how to use software, manage files, understanding qualitative and quantitative data,  or navigate online platforms, expanded into something far more complex. 

At the beginning of the term, I primarily understood digital literacy through the lens of production. In my mind, programs such as Photoshop, InDesign, and Procreate allowed artists to translate ideas into visual forms efficiently.  Or, if I was interested in research, file collection,  and collaborative platforms, applications like Pinterest and Google Drive were the best for this.
However, I soon realized this understanding was largely surface-level. I was using digital tools, but I was not yet questioning how they structured the information I consumed or the work I produced.

This perspective began to shift as I engaged with concepts from the digital humanities. Rather than seeing digital tools as mere digital substitutes for paint, canvas, or paper, I began to recognize them as systems that actively shape interpretation. Tools such as Voyant, Distant Viewing Explorer, and mapping platforms like Recogito introduced me to new ways of engaging with data, images, and texts.

For instance, Distant Viewing Explorer, which categorizes images based on visual features such as brightness, color, and detected objects, revealed how computational methods can uncover patterns in its own way. This approach resonated deeply with my interests in visual analysis. In art history, such a tool could allow for the identification of recurring visual motifs across large collections of images, offering a new method for understanding stylistic trends or symbolic repetition. Personally, I began to imagine how this kind of analysis could be applied to my own artistic practice. By examining patterns across my body of work, I could identify recurring symbols, themes, or aesthetic tendencies, insights that might otherwise be intuitive or unconscious.

Similarly, tools like Voyant expanded my understanding of textual analysis. Initially, I approached Voyant as a basic way to identify word frequency within a corpus. However, I came to see its potential for visualizing relationships within texts, mapping patterns and clusters through multiple variations based on their colors, spatial distribution, shape, among other factors. As someone interested in both writing and visual art, this intersection of data and visualization feels particularly compelling. It suggests the possibility of transforming research itself into an artistic practice: not just analyzing data, but presenting it in ways that are visually and conceptually meaningful.

Another project that significantly influenced my thinking was Automatic on the Road, in which an AI system generated a narrative based on sounds and conversations recorded during a road trip. What struck me most about this project was not simply the novelty of AI-generated text, but the methodology behind it. The project reframed data collection as a creative act. Ambient sound, overheard dialogue, and environmental noise became raw material for storytelling.

Initially, I would only consider tools like Google Docs, Microsoft Word, or Notes for support on drafting, organization, and revision of text. However, this project challenged my assumptions about authorship and creative process. It suggested that storytelling does not have to always be formatted in the traditional sense; it can emerge from different systems that capture, translate, and recombine data. For my own practice, this opens up exciting possibilities. I began to consider how I might incorporate unconventional forms of data collection, audio recordings, environmental data, or even algorithmically generated inputs, into my work.


At the same time, my engagement with generative AI tools has introduced a level of critical thinking when it comes to digital literacy.  On one hand, AI offers new modes of production and experimentation; on the other, it raises ethical and philosophical questions. Issues of authorship, bias, and originality become increasingly complex when working with systems trained on vast datasets of existing work.
After much reflection, I have come to see AI not as a replacement for human creativity, but as a medium  that must be approached with both curiosity and caution. While AI systems continue to become more sophisticated, their development is not only the result of technical updates or new features, but also of continuous human input. In many ways, AI learns from us: from the questions we ask, the patterns we reinforce, and the ways we communicate and interpret the world. This makes it both powerful and inherently limited.

Despite its many strengths, there are aspects of human experience that AI cannot fully replicate such as emotions, lived experiences, the depth of personal reflections, and all that often drives meaningful artistic work. At the same time, it is undeniable that AI is becoming increasingly efficient, capable of handling repetitive or time-consuming tasks with speed and precision. When used thoughtfully, this creates an opportunity to shift our focus toward what matters most: the conceptual, emotional, and critical dimensions of creative practice that remain uniquely human.


For this reason, I don’t seek to rely entirely on AI, but to understand how it functions, what needs to grow and to know what things must be fixed without changing my voice, and how it might be effectively used as a tool for different disciplines. Maintaining this balance feels essential to producing work that is both more cohesive and structurally improved while being  honest to the core of the creator.
In fact, one of the most valuable insights I have gained is that digital literacy is not about mastering every tool or technique. It is about developing the capacity to learn, adapt, and think critically across different systems. This shift in mindset has made the prospect of engaging with more technical skills—such as coding platforms, data visualization, or computational analysis—feel less intimidating and more generative.

Equally important is the role of digital image collections and computational analysis platforms in shaping how I conduct visual research. Tools such as Photogrammar, Orange Data Mining, and the WCMA Digital Project expand what it means to engage with images, not just as containers of memories or objects, but as datasets that can be explored, categorized, and interpreted at scale. For instance, Orange Data Mining introduces a more systematic approach to visual and data analysis, encouraging me to think in terms of patterns, clustering, and relationships rather than purely aesthetic judgment.
In the future, I am interested in exploring computational creativity more deeply. I want to develop skills in coding, such as Visual Studio Code and p5 as a way of creating more interactive and responsive works. I am also drawn to data visualization as both a research tool and an artistic medium. The idea of translating complex datasets into visual or narrative forms feels like a natural extension of my interdisciplinary interests, allowing me to bridge my work in visual art and creative writing while continuing to expand my understanding of digital literacies.

And just as I did with Recognito, I want to engage with questions of how to create collaborations with other students or colleagues across different disciplines to gather and annotate data to, not just map locations, but visualize the information in accordance with the aesthetics and final outline I wish to translate to. This includes experimenting and critically examining these tool’s implications.


Ultimately, my understanding of digital literacies has evolved from a focus on tools to a focus on the development, the different transitions from integration of material, whether textual, visual, or spatial to interpretation, and from individual practice to collaborative and interdisciplinary engagement.  As I continue my studies, I hope to remain open to new methods, technologies, and perspectives. Digital literacy, I have come to realize, is not a fixed skillset but an ongoing process of learning and adaptation. It is a way of navigating an increasingly complex creative landscape—one that requires both technical competence and critical awareness.
And perhaps most importantly, it is a way of expanding what is possible: in art, in storytelling, and in the ways we understand and represent the world around us.
